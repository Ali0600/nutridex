# Does kiwifruit help constipation?

"Two kiwifruit a day" circulates as folk advice for sluggish digestion, and it has been tested more
seriously than most food remedies — including head-to-head against psyllium, a standard fiber
laxative. A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis pooled those trials. The result is a good
example of a finding that points somewhere useful while stopping well short of proof.

## The proposed mechanism

Kiwifruit's fiber holds an unusual amount of water for its weight. Fiber that swells rather than
dissolves adds bulk and softens stool, which is the same basic lever psyllium pulls. Kiwifruit also
carries **actinidin**, a protein-digesting enzyme found in few other foods; it is best documented for
[speeding protein breakdown in the stomach](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20232890/) (Kaur et al.,
*J Agric Food Chem* 2010), and it is a plausible but not yet settled contributor to how quickly food
moves through the gut. Treat the mechanism as the reason the trials were worth running, not as
evidence in its own right.

## What the pooled trials found

A systematic review of **seven randomized controlled trials (399 participants, 82% female, mean age
42)** compared kiwifruit or kiwifruit extract against placebo or another treatment in people with
constipation, and graded the certainty of each result using GRADE ([Eltorki, Leong & Ratcliffe,
*Can J Gastroenterol Hepatol* 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36247043/)):

- **Bowel-movement frequency vs placebo:** kiwifruit extract *might* add about **1.4 spontaneous
  bowel movements per week** — but the confidence interval crossed zero, and the authors rated the
  certainty **low**. This is the headline claim, and it is the one the pooled data supports least
  firmly.
- **Vs psyllium:** kiwifruit may increase weekly bowel movements by about **1.0** (moderate
  certainty, interval again brushing zero) and scored higher on the Bristol Stool Scale, meaning
  **softer stools** — that one was statistically significant but rated *low* certainty. Roughly
  speaking, kiwifruit held its own against an established fiber supplement.
- **Abdominal pain:** reduced, at **moderate** certainty — the most solid finding in the review.
- **Straining:** improved on average, but not to a degree the pooled data could distinguish from
  chance.
- **Side effects:** encapsulated kiwifruit *extract* showed a signal toward more minor adverse
  events than placebo, though the estimate was too imprecise to call.

The authors' own conclusion is the honest summary: overall **low certainty** that kiwifruit increases
bowel movements versus placebo or psyllium, promising enough to warrant large, rigorous trials.

## The honest caveats

- **Low certainty is not a formality.** Most of the pooled estimates ran in kiwifruit's favor while
  including "no effect" within their range. That is a hint, not a demonstration.
- **Seven small trials, 399 people**, mostly women in their early forties. Nothing here speaks to
  children, older adults, or constipation from a diagnosed cause.
- **Fruit and capsule are not the same intervention.** Several trials used extracts; the whole-fruit
  results and the extract results are pooled together, and the possible side-effect signal was on the
  extract side.
- Persistent constipation, or constipation with bleeding, weight loss, or a sudden change in habit,
  is a reason to see a clinician — not a reason to eat more fruit and wait.

## The takeaway

Kiwifruit is a reasonable, low-risk thing to try for mild constipation: it performed comparably to
psyllium in the trials that ran that comparison, and it reduced abdominal pain with moderate
certainty. Just hold the expectation loosely — the evidence is genuinely thin, and the review's
authors say so plainly. Read the full entry on [kiwifruit](/items/kiwi), or browse the other [foods
for gut & digestion](/goals/gut-health).
